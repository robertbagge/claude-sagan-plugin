# Symbolic / Rule-Based NLP (1960s–1980s)

For roughly three decades, the dominant bet in computational language work was that human language could be captured as a system of formal rules — grammars over symbols, logical representations of meaning, and hand-curated knowledge — and that, given enough such rules, a machine could parse, understand, and answer. That bet produced the field's first famous demos (ELIZA, SHRDLU, LUNAR), its first formal grammar machinery (ATNs, LFG, HPSG), and its first wave of commercial expert systems. It also produced the frustration that, by the late 1980s, sent the field reaching for probabilities and corpora instead.

This dossier walks the arc: the assumptions, the canonical systems, the formal-grammar superstructure, and the ceiling that the symbolic paradigm hit.

## The Foundational Bet: Language as Logic Over Symbols

The symbolic paradigm grew out of a specific intellectual posture, made formal by Chomsky in the 1950s: a sentence is a structured object generated by rules, and *linguistic competence* — the speaker's tacit knowledge of those rules — is the proper object of study. Chomsky's 1956 paper "Three Models for the Description of Language" classified grammars into a hierarchy and located natural-language syntax inside the context-free (Type 2) tier, with transformations layered on top [T2] Wikipedia, "Context-free grammar," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context-free_grammar (accessed 2026-05). The same formalism, transmitted through John Backus and the Algol project, became Backus–Naur Form for programming languages [T2] same source. So computer science and linguistics inherited a *shared vocabulary* in the early 1960s: nonterminals, productions, parse trees.

That shared vocabulary made one assumption look natural: if a CFG can specify Algol exactly, a richer rule system ought to be able to specify English. From that premise, the field's research program followed almost mechanically — write the grammar, write the parser, write the semantic interpreter, plug in a knowledge base, and you have understanding. Meaning was treated as something compositional and logical: predicates over entities, encoded in a formal representation that a theorem-prover or a planner could chew on.

Two further assumptions came along for the ride. First, *competence over performance*: messy speech, ungrammatical input, and human inference shortcuts were treated as noise around a clean rule system. Second, *knowledge as hand-built rules*: if the system needed to know that birds fly, somebody would write `fly(X) :- bird(X)`, plus the exceptions. Both assumptions look obviously wrong in 2026 hindsight. They did not look obviously wrong in 1968.

## ELIZA (1966): Pattern Matching Without Understanding

Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA, published in *Communications of the ACM* in January 1966, is the canonical first chatbot — and, importantly, the canonical first lesson in how easy it is to *look* like understanding. ELIZA ran on the MIT MAC time-sharing system and worked entirely by surface pattern matching: input sentences were analyzed by *decomposition rules* triggered on keywords, and responses were assembled by *reassembly rules* paired to those decompositions [T1] Weizenbaum 1966, *Comm. ACM* 9(1), pp. 36–45, ¶ describing the decomposition/reassembly architecture, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/365153.365168.

A famous example: a template matched any sentence of the form "I am X" and reassembled it as "How long have you been X" — *independently of the meaning of X* [T1] Weizenbaum 1966, p. 37, example template. Critically, the keywords and rules were not part of the program; they were a *script*, loaded as data. The most famous script, DOCTOR, performed a parody of Rogerian psychotherapy, where reflecting questions back at the patient is, by design, the move [T2] CACM republication, "ELIZA—a computer program for the study of natural language communication between man and machine," https://cacm.acm.org/research/eliza-a-computer-program-for-the-study-of-natural-language-communication-between-man-and-machine-2/.

ELIZA shipped about 200 lines of MAD-SLIP and a script of perhaps a few hundred patterns. It had no model of the user, no memory beyond a few keyword stacks, no representation of meaning. Yet users — including, famously, Weizenbaum's secretary — formed emotional attachments and asked to be left alone with it. Weizenbaum spent the rest of his career deeply uncomfortable about this. The "ELIZA effect" — humans projecting understanding onto trivially shallow systems — is the quiet, durable lesson of the symbolic era; LLM observers in 2026 will recognize it.

## SHRDLU (1972): Understanding That Worked, In a Universe With Eight Blocks

If ELIZA was the cautionary tale, SHRDLU was the existence proof. Terry Winograd's MIT thesis system, developed 1968–1970 and popularized through his 1972 book *Understanding Natural Language*, conducted typed dialogues with a user about a simulated tabletop of coloured blocks and pyramids [T2] Wikipedia, "SHRDLU," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SHRDLU (accessed 2026-05). It could parse "Find a block which is taller than the one you are holding and put it into the box" and *execute* the action, then answer "Why did you do that?" with a coherent justification [T2] same source.

Three things made SHRDLU look like a giant leap. First, it integrated syntax (a procedural grammar in MICRO-PLANNER), semantics, and a planner over a world model — meaning was *grounded* in things the simulated arm could do. Second, it handled anaphora ("the cone" = the green cone we just discussed) using a tiny dialogue memory [T2] same source. Third, it could explain itself by walking back through its own goal stack. This was natural-language understanding that didn't merely parrot.

The trouble was the world. SHRDLU's vocabulary covered roughly 50 words — nouns like "block" and "cone," verbs like "place on," adjectives like "big" and "blue" [T2] Wikipedia, "SHRDLU." Adding a new object type meant editing the grammar, the world model, and the planner together; complexity grew superlinearly with each addition [T3] Brenndoerfer, "SHRDLU — Understanding Language Through Action," https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/history-shrdlu-language-understanding-blocks-world. Winograd himself would later call SHRDLU something close to a Potemkin village — impressive at demo length, fundamentally unable to scale [T3] Brenndoerfer, same URL. By the mid-1980s, after long conversations with Hubert Dreyfus and Fernando Flores, Winograd had publicly walked away from classical symbolic AI, publishing the phenomenology-flavoured *Understanding Computers and Cognition* (with Flores) in 1986 [T2] Wikipedia, "Terry Winograd," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Winograd.

That trajectory — *spectacular toy demo → cannot generalize → author defects* — is the symbolic era in miniature.

## LUNAR (1973) and the Question-Answering Front

Bill Woods' LUNAR system, built at BBN under NASA contract, took the symbolic dream into a real domain: it answered English questions about the chemical analyses of Apollo 11 moon rocks for working geologists [T2] Wikipedia, "William Aaron Woods," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Aaron_Woods. LUNAR translated questions into a formal query language over a relational database of lunar samples; it could compute averages, ratios, and references against a keyphrase index [T1] Woods 1973, "Progress in natural language understanding: an application to lunar geology," AFIPS Conference Proceedings 42, pp. 441–450, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1499586.1499695.

The often-cited demonstration number: at a 1971 lunar science convention LUNAR answered roughly 90% of in-domain questions posed by people who had never used the system [T2] Wikipedia, "William Aaron Woods" (citing Woods' demo). That looks like triumph. The asterisk is "in-domain": LUNAR's coverage was scoped to a defined database and a defined task. The system never grew beyond its prototype: every new domain required a new grammar, a new lexicon, and a new mapping into the query language, with no obvious reuse path. LUNAR was, in retrospect, a working prototype of what would later become "natural-language interfaces to databases" — useful, narrow, and brittle outside its niche.

## The Formal-Grammar Industrial Complex: ATNs, LFG, HPSG

Through the 1970s and 1980s, computational linguists put serious mathematical effort into *grammar formalisms* — the machinery that would, in principle, let you specify English the way Backus had specified Algol. Three threads stand out.

**Augmented Transition Networks (ATNs).** Bill Woods, again, in 1970: extend a finite-state network with recursion (so it could call sub-networks for noun phrases inside sentences) and *registers* that store partial constituents and run tests/actions during parsing [T1] Woods 1970, "Transition network grammars for natural language analysis," *Comm. ACM* 13(10), pp. 591–606, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/355598.362773. ATNs let parsers build sentence representations distinct from the surface text — capturing, e.g., the underlying argument structure of a passive sentence — and they were the dominant practical NLP parser of the 1970s [T2] Wikipedia, "Augmented transition network," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented_transition_network.

**Lexical-Functional Grammar (LFG).** In Joan Bresnan and Ron Kaplan's 1982 paper "Lexical-Functional Grammar: A Formal System for Grammatical Representation," and in Bresnan's edited volume *The Mental Representation of Grammatical Relations* the same year, LFG split syntactic representation into two parallel levels: *c-structure* (constituent / phrase structure) and *f-structure* (functional relations like SUBJECT, OBJECT, TENSE), linked by unification of attribute–value matrices [T2] Dalrymple, "Lexical-Functional Grammar: An Overview," *Annual Review of Linguistics*, https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-062419-125014. Bresnan and Kaplan were explicitly chasing *psychological plausibility and computational tractability* — i.e., a grammar that a finite mind could acquire and a real machine could parse [T2] Neidle, "Lexical Functional Grammar," Boston University, https://www.bu.edu/asllrp/neidle-lfg.pdf.

**Head-Driven Phrase Structure Grammar (HPSG).** Carl Pollard and Ivan Sag's mid-1980s framework, formally laid out in *Information-Based Syntax and Semantics* (1987) and *Head-Driven Phrase Structure Grammar* (1994), pushed *lexicalism* further: most grammatical information lives in rich, typed feature structures attached to lexical entries, and a small set of universal principles plus unification does the rest of the work [T2] Wikipedia, "Head-driven phrase structure grammar," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head-driven_phrase_structure_grammar. HPSG drew on Categorial Grammar, GPSG, and LFG, and was explicitly framed as cognitive-science-friendly [T2] same source.

These were serious intellectual achievements. They are also, today, mostly absent from production NLP. The reason is structural: every one of them required armies of trained linguists to specify lexicons and rules, and every one of them broke when faced with the long tail of real language — disfluencies, jargon, novel coinages, idioms, ungrammatical input that humans handle effortlessly.

## Schank, Conceptual Dependency, and the Knowledge Move

A parallel tradition, mostly out of Yale, decided that syntax was largely a distraction and that the real problem was *meaning representation*. Roger Schank introduced Conceptual Dependency (CD) theory in 1969 and laid it out in detail in "Conceptual Dependency: A Theory of Natural Language Understanding" in *Cognitive Psychology* in 1972 [T1] Schank 1972, *Cognitive Psychology* 3(4), pp. 552–631, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0010028572900229. CD reduced verbs to a small inventory of *primitive acts* — ATRANS (transfer of possession, e.g., "give"), PTRANS (physical transfer, e.g., "go"), MTRANS (mental transfer, e.g., "tell"), and so on — with relations between concepts expressed as dependencies [T2] Wikipedia, "Conceptual dependency theory," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_dependency_theory. The bet: two sentences with different surface form but the same meaning should produce the same CD representation, and inference would happen at that level.

Schank and Robert Abelson then layered *scripts* — stereotyped event sequences like "going to a restaurant" — on top of CD, as the structure that fills in the inferences a story leaves implicit [T1] Schank & Abelson 1975, "Scripts, plans, and knowledge," IJCAI proceedings, https://home.mis.u-picardie.fr/~furst/docs/Schank_Abelson_Scripts_1975.pdf. By 1975 this had produced SAM (Script Applier Mechanism), which used scripts to read and answer questions about short newspaper-style stories [T2] Wikipedia, "Conceptual dependency theory."

The ambition was huge — extract the gist of a story, fill in unstated steps — but the engineering was punishing: every domain needed its own hand-written scripts, and the scripts themselves couldn't handle scenarios that deviated from the stereotype. The restaurant script, the canonical example, makes the field's deepest unsolved problem visible: *you cannot enumerate the variations*.

## Expert Systems: The Commercial Wave That Crashed

Adjacent to the NLP work, the 1970s and 1980s saw the *expert system* — symbolic AI's commercial export. Two flagship systems anchor the story. **DENDRAL**, started at Stanford in 1965 by Edward Feigenbaum, Bruce Buchanan, Joshua Lederberg, and Carl Djerassi, inferred molecular structures from mass-spectrometry data via a hand-built rule base over chemistry knowledge [T2] "Early AI Systems: DENDRAL and MYCIN," Redress Compliance, https://redresscompliance.com/early-ai-systems-dendral-and-mycin/. **MYCIN**, built by Edward Shortliffe at Stanford in the early 1970s, used backward chaining over roughly 600 if-then rules to recommend antibiotics for blood infections [T2] Wikipedia, "Mycin," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycin; [T2] Buchanan & Shortliffe 1984, *Rule-Based Expert Systems: The MYCIN Experiments*, ch. 1, https://www.shortliffe.net/Buchanan-Shortliffe-1984/Chapter-01.pdf.

Through the 1980s, this paradigm became an industry — XCON at DEC, expert-system shells, Lisp machines, the Japanese Fifth Generation project. Then it collapsed. The failure mode was consistent: rules were expensive to elicit from experts, expensive to maintain, and *brittle* — they made grotesque mistakes on inputs slightly outside their distribution, and they could not learn [T2] Wikipedia, "Expert system," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expert_system. Lisp machines were obsoleted by general-purpose workstations. By 1987–1993, AI funding had collapsed into the *Second AI Winter* [T2] Wikipedia, "AI winter," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_winter.

The earlier *First AI Winter* had been seeded in 1973 by Sir James Lighthill's report to the UK Science Research Council, which — invited to evaluate AI — concluded that the field had failed its grandiose objectives, calling out *combinatorial explosion* (the intractability of search over symbolic states as problems scale) as the core, structural defect [T2] Wikipedia, "AI winter." UK AI funding was gutted. NLP, riding the same paradigm, took the same hit.

## The Ceiling: Why Symbolic NLP Stalled

The autopsy of the symbolic era keeps returning the same four causes.

1. **Lexical and structural ambiguity.** "I saw the man with the telescope." A CFG generates both readings; nothing in pure syntax prefers one. Resolving such ambiguity requires *world knowledge* — and world knowledge could not be hand-coded fast enough. Cyc, Doug Lenat's project to enumerate commonsense knowledge by hand, started in 1984 and is still going; that timeline is itself the indictment.

2. **The long tail.** Every grammar covers the centre of the distribution and breaks on the tail: ellipsis, dislocation, dialect, slang, ungrammatical-but-clear utterances. Real text has a long tail of constructions, and no human team can write rules for all of them.

3. **Brittleness and combinatorial explosion.** Expert systems and rule-based parsers degraded catastrophically — not gracefully — when their assumptions were violated [T2] Wikipedia, "AI winter." Adding a rule to fix one case usually broke another. Lighthill's intractability point applied directly: search and inference over richer symbol systems blew up [T3] AI Innovations Unleashed, "Deep Dive: The 1973 Lighthill Report," https://www.aiinnovationsunleashed.com/deep-dive-the-1973-lighthill-report-how-one-mathematician-accidentally-triggered-ais-dark-age/.

4. **No learning.** Symbolic systems could not improve from data. Every improvement required a human in the loop writing more rules. By the mid-1980s, two things were converging: cheap compute and the appearance of large machine-readable corpora (the Brown Corpus from 1964 had been a curiosity; the Penn Treebank, started 1989, would change the game).

The pivotal vibe shift came out of speech recognition, which had been quietly running on hidden Markov models and information theory since the 1970s. Frederick Jelinek, who ran IBM's speech-recognition group from 1972, became the symbol of the change with his (probably apocryphal but endlessly repeated) line — claimed in his own 2004 retelling to have been delivered at a 1988 NLP-evaluation workshop — "Every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recognizer goes up" [T2] Wikipedia, "Frederick Jelinek," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Jelinek; [T3] Hirschberg 1998 invited talk, "Every time I fire a linguist…," AAAI-98, referenced in Wikipedia entry. By the early 1990s — with DARPA funding shifting to evaluation-driven, statistics-friendly methodology, and IBM's Candide MT system showing that an n-gram and an alignment model could outperform decades of rule-engineering [T2] ETHW, "The History of Natural Language Processing," https://ethw.org/The_History_of_Natural_Language_Processing — the field's centre of gravity moved decisively toward probability and corpora.

That move is the next chapter. The symbolic era didn't disappear: dependency parsing, FrameNet, WordNet, and modern semantic-role labelling all carry symbolic DNA. Type-theoretic and constraint-based ideas are still alive in formal semantics. And the ELIZA-effect lesson — that humans will project understanding onto any plausibly fluent surface, *especially* when the system has none — is arguably more relevant in 2026 than it was in 1966.
