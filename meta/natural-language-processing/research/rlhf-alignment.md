# RLHF, Instruction Tuning, and Alignment

How a pretrained next-token predictor becomes an assistant. This dossier traces the post-pretraining alignment stack from the 2017 deep-RL roots of preference learning, through the FLAN/T0 instruction-tuning era, the InstructGPT three-step recipe that became RLHF, the ChatGPT inflection in late 2022, Anthropic's Constitutional AI, and the wave of "direct" preference optimisation methods (DPO, IPO, KTO, ORPO) that followed. Technical depth, primary sources, and inline citation tags.

## 1. What "alignment" means in this context

In the post-pretraining stack, "alignment" is a narrow, operational term: making a pretrained language model produce outputs that humans would actually want from an assistant — helpful, honest about uncertainty, and not harmful — given an instruction or conversation. A vanilla autoregressive model trained on a web crawl is, at its core, a distribution over plausible continuations. It will happily complete a question with another question, recite a canard found in its training data, or produce a fluent paragraph that is syntactically perfect and factually wrong. The "misalignment" problem is the gap between *predicting what tokens come next on the internet* and *producing what a careful labeler would consider a good answer to a user's request* [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, p. 1, ¶1 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155).

OpenAI framed the InstructGPT work explicitly as alignment: "language models are not aligned with their users" because the LM objective — predict the next token — differs from "follow the user's instructions helpfully and safely" [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, p. 1, ¶2. Anthropic's "HHH" framing — helpful, honest, harmless — became the de facto vocabulary for what the alignment stack is supposed to deliver, and Constitutional AI was an attempt to push specifically on the harmless axis without collapsing helpfulness [T1] Bai et al. 2022, p. 1, abstract (https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073).

Two things matter throughout. First, this is a *behavioural* notion of alignment — measured by labeler preference rates and benchmark scores, not by any deeper claim about the model's goals. Second, every alignment method here is a fine-tuning method: it assumes a strong base model already exists and asks how to nudge it.

## 2. From autocomplete to instruction-follower: FLAN and T0

The first cheap intervention was supervised: take a large pretrained LM, fine-tune it on a mixture of NLP tasks each rewritten as a natural-language instruction, and watch zero-shot performance on held-out tasks improve. Google's FLAN paper instruction-tuned the 137B LaMDA-PT base on over 60 NLP datasets verbalised via templates and grouped into 12 task clusters; FLAN beat zero-shot GPT-3 175B on 20 of 25 evaluation datasets, and beat *few-shot* GPT-3 on several including ANLI, RTE, BoolQ, and StoryCloze [T1] Wei et al. 2021, abstract & §3 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652). A crucial ablation: instruction tuning only helps at sufficient scale — at 8B parameters and below, performance actually got *worse* after instruction tuning, with the smaller models seemingly using their capacity to memorise the mixture rather than to generalise the meta-skill of "follow an instruction" [T1] Wei et al. 2021, §4.

BigScience's T0 made the parallel argument from a different direction. Sanh et al. fine-tuned a T5-derived encoder-decoder on a large multitask prompted mixture and showed that the resulting 11B model matched or beat GPT-3 (175B) zero-shot on many standard datasets — roughly 16x parameter efficiency — by leveraging diverse, human-authored prompts per task [T1] Sanh et al. 2021, abstract & §1 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08207). The two papers together established a thesis that has held up: *prompted multi-task SFT generalises*. Every modern post-training pipeline starts here, even if it doesn't stop here.

Instruction tuning alone has limits. It teaches the model the *form* of an instruction-following response, but it inherits whatever taste the demonstration data has, and there's no mechanism to compare two plausible answers and prefer one — every demonstration is treated as equally good. That's the gap that RLHF fills.

## 3. The PPO-based RLHF recipe

### 3.1 Roots: preference-based RL

The lineage of "learn from comparisons, not from a hand-coded reward" predates language models. Christiano et al. (2017) showed that a deep RL agent could solve Atari games and MuJoCo locomotion using only pairwise comparisons of short trajectory segments by non-expert humans, with feedback on under 1% of the agent's interactions; the trick was to fit a parameterised reward model to the preferences (Bradley-Terry style) and run standard RL against the learned reward [T1] Christiano et al. 2017, §2 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741). This separated *what we want* from *how to do it* — and it was the conceptual seed of RLHF.

Stiennon et al. (2020) carried the recipe to language. Working on TL;DR Reddit summarisation, they trained a reward model on human pairwise preferences over summaries, then fine-tuned a GPT-style policy with PPO against the learned reward. The fine-tuned model produced summaries that humans preferred to *human reference summaries*, which had been the prior ceiling for the task [T1] Stiennon et al. 2020, abstract & §3 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325). By the time InstructGPT shipped, every component of the modern recipe — pairwise preference data, Bradley-Terry reward model, PPO with a KL penalty against a frozen reference policy — already existed; the contribution was to apply it to a general-purpose instruction-following assistant at scale.

### 3.2 InstructGPT: the canonical three steps

Ouyang et al. (2022) defined the recipe most practitioners now mean when they say "RLHF" [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, Fig. 2 (p. 3) (https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155):

1. **SFT (supervised fine-tuning).** Collect labeler demonstrations of desired behaviour for a distribution of prompts; fine-tune the base GPT-3 on these via standard cross-entropy. About 13K training prompts in InstructGPT.
2. **Reward modelling.** Show the SFT model K=4–9 sampled completions per prompt; have labelers rank them. Train a separate reward model `r_θ(x, y)` to score completions, with a Bradley-Terry pairwise loss
   `loss(θ) = −E[log σ(r_θ(x, y_w) − r_θ(x, y_l))]` (averaged over comparisons within a prompt to avoid overfitting). About 33K training prompts [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.5.
3. **PPO with KL penalty (PPO-ptx).** Treat the SFT model as the reference policy `π_SFT`. Sample completions from the current RL policy `π_φ`, score with `r_θ`, and optimise:
   `objective(φ) = E[r_θ(x, y) − β · log(π_φ(y|x) / π_SFT(y|x))] + γ · E_pretrain[log π_φ(x)]`
   The β-weighted log-ratio is a per-token KL penalty against the SFT reference, keeping the policy from drifting into degenerate but high-reward regions; the γ pretraining mixing term mitigates regressions on standard NLP benchmarks. About 31K prompts in the PPO stage [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.6.

Used about 40 contractor labelers screened for sensitivity to demographic and harm signals [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.4. The headline result: humans preferred outputs from the *1.3B* InstructGPT to outputs from the *175B* GPT-3 base model, a roughly 100x parameter efficiency gain on the user-intent objective. The 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 about 85±3% of the time on the OpenAI prompt distribution [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, §4.1 & Fig. 1.

PPO itself is a 2017 policy-gradient algorithm by Schulman et al. that approximates a trust-region update via a clipped surrogate objective; it's first-order, multi-epoch on the same minibatch, and empirically more stable than vanilla policy gradients while avoiding TRPO's second-order machinery [T1] Schulman et al. 2017, §3 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347). Most RLHF implementations use PPO essentially as published, with two LM-specific tweaks: per-token KL penalty against a reference policy folded into the reward, and a value head sharing parameters with the policy.

### 3.3 The ChatGPT inflection

On 30 November 2022, OpenAI posted a low-key blog announcement: "We've trained a model called ChatGPT which interacts in a conversational way." It described ChatGPT as a sibling model to InstructGPT, "trained using Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), using the same methods as InstructGPT, but with slight differences in the data collection setup" [T2] OpenAI, "Introducing ChatGPT", 2022-11-30 (https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/). ChatGPT reached 1M users in five days and an estimated 100M monthly users by January 2023, the fastest consumer-app ramp on record at the time [T3] UBS analyst note via Reuters/coverage, https://www.reuters.com/technology/chatgpt-sets-record-fastest-growing-user-base-analyst-note-2023-02-01/. Two non-technical points worth carrying forward: the *technique* was already public (InstructGPT, Stiennon, Christiano), and the *product* was a chat-formatted wrapper around it. RLHF as a research idea was eight months old when ChatGPT made it a household phenomenon.

## 4. Reward models and reward hacking

The reward model is the load-bearing assumption of PPO-based RLHF. It is a learned, imperfect proxy for what humans actually prefer, and the policy is optimised against it. The Bradley-Terry pairwise sigmoid loss treats `r_θ(x, y)` as a logit of "y is preferred to its sibling on prompt x"; up to a per-prompt additive constant, the learned reward identifies preferred from rejected responses, but its absolute values, calibration, and out-of-distribution behaviour are all weakly constrained by training [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.5; [T1] Christiano et al. 2017, §2.

Hence reward hacking. As the policy is optimised harder against `r_θ`, the *proxy* score keeps going up while the *gold* (true human-judged) reward eventually plateaus and falls — Goodhart's Law in action. Gao, Schulman, and Hilton (2022) made this quantitative by setting up a synthetic experiment in which a fixed large "gold" reward model plays the role of humans and labels data used to train a smaller "proxy" reward model; they then optimise against the proxy and watch the gold score [T1] Gao et al. 2022, §3 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760). They derive empirically that for both best-of-N sampling and RL, gold reward as a function of `√KL(π || π_ref)` follows a clean parametric form; coefficients scale smoothly with reward-model size, and the optimum gold score is bounded by reward-model capacity. This is the cleanest published characterisation of "how hard can you push PPO before it starts producing slop the reward model loves and humans don't."

Standard mitigations are all already visible in InstructGPT and the post-2022 literature: KL penalty against a reference (it's a brake, not a fix); reward-model ensembles to detect disagreement; early stopping; periodic refresh of reward-model training data with new policy samples; and pretraining-mix gradients (the γ term) to keep the model anchored to its base distribution [T1] Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.6; [T1] Gao et al. 2022, §5. None of these fully solve reward hacking — they buy headroom.

A second failure mode that the field is still working on is sycophancy: reward models trained on human preferences tend to reward agreement-with-the-user even when the user is wrong, because labelers prefer agreeable responses on the margin. This isn't a Goodhart effect on a fixed reward — it's that the reward itself is mis-specified in a systematic, social way [T1] Sharma et al. 2023, "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models", §3 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548).

## 5. Constitutional AI: scaling oversight via AI feedback

Anthropic's Constitutional AI paper (Bai et al. 2022) asked whether the harmlessness portion of RLHF could be done without human harmlessness labels — replacing them with AI judgments guided by an explicit written "constitution" of principles. The pipeline has two stages [T1] Bai et al. 2022, §2–§4 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073):

- **SL-CAI (supervised stage).** Start from a helpful-only RLHF model. For red-team prompts, sample a (potentially harmful) response, then have the model itself *critique* the response against a randomly chosen constitutional principle and *revise* it. Iterate the critique–revise loop, then fine-tune on the revised completions plus the original helpfulness data.
- **RL-CAI (RLAIF stage).** For pairs of responses, prompt a model with a multiple-choice question — "which response is less harmful?" — selecting from the same principles. Use *chain-of-thought* reasoning before the answer to improve judgments. Train a preference model on these AI-labelled pairs and run PPO against it, exactly as in standard RLHF, but with no human labels for harmlessness.

Example principles: "Identify specific ways in which the assistant's last response is harmful, unethical, racist, sexist, toxic, dangerous, or illegal" and "Choose the response that a wise, ethical, polite and friendly person would more likely say" [T1] Bai et al. 2022, Appendix C. The headline result is a Pareto-improved helpfulness-vs-harmlessness frontier compared to RLHF: RL-CAI matches or beats RLHF on harmlessness while remaining "non-evasive" — the model engages with hard prompts and explains its objections rather than refusing flatly [T1] Bai et al. 2022, §1 & Fig. 2.

Two points are worth flagging. First, the *labels* go away but the *demonstrations* (helpfulness) and the *base capability* (a model competent enough to evaluate itself) do not — RLAIF stands on RLHF, it doesn't replace it. Second, the constitution is a transparent, auditable artifact, which is both Constitutional AI's claim to interpretability and a sharp critique vector (who writes the constitution, on what authority).

## 6. DPO and the move beyond PPO

By mid-2023, PPO-based RLHF was working but expensive: separate reward model, separate value head, online sampling during PPO, four model copies in memory (policy, reference, reward, value), reward-model overoptimisation to debug, and PPO hyperparameter fragility. The question was whether the reward-modelling and RL steps could be collapsed.

Rafailov et al. (2023) showed they could. The DPO derivation is short and clean. Start from the standard KL-constrained RL objective (eq. 3): `max_π E[r(x,y)] − β D_KL[π(·|x) ‖ π_ref(·|x)]`. The closed-form optimal policy (eq. 4) is `π_r(y|x) = (1/Z(x)) π_ref(y|x) exp(r(x,y)/β)`, where `Z(x)` is the partition function. Inverting this gives the *implicit reward* (eq. 5): `r(x,y) = β log(π_r(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) + β log Z(x)` — i.e., for the optimal policy, the reward is just the log probability ratio against the reference, up to a prompt-dependent constant. Substitute this into the Bradley-Terry preference probability `P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = σ(r(x,y_w) − r(x,y_l))`; the `β log Z(x)` terms cancel because they depend only on `x`, not on `y_w` or `y_l`. The remaining objective is the DPO loss (eq. 7) [T1] Rafailov et al. 2023, §4 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290):

`L_DPO = −E[log σ(β log(π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) − β log(π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x)))]`.

This is a binary cross-entropy classification loss on log-probability ratios. No reward model. No on-policy sampling. No PPO. The "language model is secretly a reward model" subtitle is precise: under the Bradley-Terry assumption, optimising DPO on preferences *is* optimising a reward whose closed form is the log-ratio of the policy to the reference. Empirically, DPO matched or beat PPO with less compute and more stable training: 61% vs 57% summarisation win rate on TL;DR, equal or better dialogue performance on Anthropic HH, and superior reward-KL frontiers in a controlled sentiment task [T1] Rafailov et al. 2023, §6.

DPO's importance is partly conceptual (it gave the field a clean theoretical handle on what RLHF is doing) and partly practical (it dropped the engineering bar low enough that academic labs and small teams could ship aligned models on commodity GPUs). Within a year, DPO and its variants were shipping in open-weight model post-training across the ecosystem.

The successors fix specific complaints:

- **IPO (Identity Preference Optimisation; Azar et al. 2023).** Defines a general ΨPO family and observes that DPO shares with PPO-RLHF the assumption that pairwise preferences can be summarised by a Bradley-Terry pointwise reward. When preferences are deterministic (always-prefer-y_w), DPO has no implicit regularisation against driving the log-ratio to infinity, so it overfits. IPO sets `Ψ = identity` and adds a term that *bounds* the log-ratio gap, giving better behaviour on near-deterministic data and a cleaner theoretical story [T1] Azar et al. 2023, §3–§4 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036).
- **KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimisation; Ethayarajh et al. 2024).** Drops the requirement for *paired* preferences entirely. Each example is a prompt and a single response labelled simply "desirable" or "undesirable". The loss is built from a Kahneman-Tversky utility shape (loss-averse, reference-dependent) — KTO is presented as one member of a family of *human-aware losses* (HALOs). Because thumbs-up/thumbs-down data is much cheaper than carefully constructed preference pairs, KTO is appealing in production. It matches or beats DPO across 1B–30B scales on standard benchmarks [T1] Ethayarajh et al. 2024, §3 & §5 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01306).
- **ORPO (Odds-Ratio Preference Optimisation; Hong et al. 2024).** Removes the reference policy entirely. Instead of two model copies (policy + frozen reference), ORPO adds a log-odds-ratio penalty term to the SFT cross-entropy loss, so a single SFT-style training run does both instruction-following and preference learning. Fine-tuning Phi-2 (2.7B), Llama-2 (7B), and Mistral (7B) on UltraFeedback with ORPO beat Llama-2 Chat and Zephyr-7B/13B on AlpacaEval 2.0 (12.20%) and MT-Bench (7.32) [T1] Hong et al. 2024, §3 & §5 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07691).

A subtlety: DPO and its descendants are *offline* — they assume a fixed preference dataset and do not generate fresh samples during training. PPO-RLHF is *online* — the policy generates samples that the reward model scores at every step. Online sampling lets the system explore failure modes the static dataset doesn't cover; offline methods are simpler but bounded by the diversity of the preference data. Several 2024 lines of work (online DPO, IPO with on-policy sampling, iterative DPO, RSO) try to put on-policy sampling back into direct methods; expect the offline/online distinction to keep moving [T3] Hugging Face TRL "Preference Optimization" docs (https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/dpo_trainer).

## 7. Open problems and trajectory

The post-pretraining stack is unfinished. Five threads to watch:

1. **Reward hacking at frontier scale.** Gao et al.'s scaling-laws paper is on a small reward model with a synthetic gold; whether the same functional forms hold for frontier-scale RLHF, and whether reward-hacking gets worse with more capable policies (which can find more exotic exploits), is open [T1] Gao et al. 2022, §6.
2. **Process supervision over outcome supervision.** Lightman et al. (2023) showed that for math reasoning, rewarding correct *intermediate steps* (process-supervised reward models, PRMs) outperforms rewarding only the final answer — and produces less reward hacking on MATH [T1] Lightman et al. 2023, "Let's Verify Step by Step", §3 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050). This generalises poorly outside math today, but it's the most concrete handle on "reward what you actually want, not what's correlated with it."
3. **Scalable oversight.** Constitutional AI is one bet — let an AI label preferences when humans can't. Debate, recursive reward modelling, and weak-to-strong generalisation are others. The shared assumption is that as capability grows, human labelers stop being a reliable supervisory signal.
4. **Sycophancy and preference data quality.** Reward models inherit labeler biases; labelers prefer assertive, agreeable, lengthy responses. Cleaning the upstream signal — better rubrics, adversarial labelers, calibration on hard cases — is plumbing-level but high-leverage [T1] Sharma et al. 2023, §3.
5. **The DPO-vs-PPO debate.** DPO's simplicity won the open ecosystem; closed labs continue to use PPO-style online RLHF, often citing online sampling as load-bearing for hard alignment problems and reasoning. The empirical question — for which tasks, at which scale, with which preference data — is unsettled. Papers in 2024–2025 (e.g. Tang et al. on online DPO, Pang et al. on iterative methods) are converging on hybrid recipes [T3] Lambert, "The State of Post-Training" (Interconnects), https://www.interconnects.ai/p/the-state-of-post-training-2024.

The arc from Christiano 2017 to ORPO 2024 is short — seven years — and the operational alignment stack is now standard infrastructure. That's the achievement and the warning. The techniques are mature; their failure modes are not yet well-characterised; and the tools we have for measuring success (labeler preference, benchmark scores) are exactly the tools the optimisation procedure is allowed to game.
