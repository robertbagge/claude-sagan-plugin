# Open Weights & the OSS Ecosystem

The open-weights movement is the most consequential structural shift in NLP since the transformer paper itself. In four years it took the ability to run a frontier-quality language model from "you need OpenAI's API and a credit card" to "you can torrent a 671B-parameter MoE that matches o1 and run distilled variants on a laptop." This dossier traces that arc — the model lineage, the infrastructure that made it possible, the licensing fights that surround it, and the regulatory regimes now trying to catch up.

## 1. Why open weights became the central debate

The closed-vs-open frame dominates AI policy because the gap is genuinely small and shrinking. Epoch AI's tracking of the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI) puts open-weight models roughly 3 months behind state-of-the-art closed models on average, with Meta's Llama 3.1 405B closing the gap across multiple benchmarks and DeepSeek-R1 trailing OpenAI's o3-mini by only 2 percentage points on MATH Level 5 [T3] Epoch AI, "Open vs. closed AI: How behind are open models?", https://epoch.ai/blog/open-models-report. The headline policy implication: "Any capabilities appearing at the frontier are likely to be widely available and unrestricted in less than a year, complicating regulatory options" [T3] Epoch AI, https://epoch.ai/data-insights/open-weights-vs-closed-weights-models.

This makes open weights both the main alternative governance regime to closed APIs and the main object of safety/regulatory anxiety. It also makes the precise meaning of "open" load-bearing — a distinction the community spent 2024 arguing over.

## 2. The lineage: BLOOM → LLaMA → Mistral/Mixtral → Llama 3/4

**BLOOM (BigScience, 2022)** is the spiritual predecessor. A 176B-parameter decoder-only transformer trained on the ROOTS corpus across 46 natural and 13 programming languages, it was the product of a year-long collaboration between hundreds of researchers, with compute provided by France's Jean Zay supercomputer; final training took 117 days [T1] Le Scao et al., "BLOOM: A 176B-Parameter Open-Access Multilingual Language Model", arXiv 2211.05100, p. 1, ¶1. BLOOM established three norms that would define the movement: full model card disclosure, a permissive "RAIL" license that distinguishes it from later restricted releases, and the principle that compute-heavy frontier training could be done in the open.

**LLaMA 1 (Meta, February 2023)** was the inflection point. Touvron et al. released a family from 7B to 65B parameters, trained exclusively on publicly available data, and showed that LLaMA-13B outperformed GPT-3 (175B) on most benchmarks while LLaMA-65B was competitive with Chinchilla-70B and PaLM-540B [T1] Touvron et al., "LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models", arXiv 2302.13971, p. 1, ¶2. The release was non-commercial-research-only, but on March 2, 2023 a 4chan user named "llamanon" posted the 7B and 65B weights as a torrent; mirrors appeared on GitHub and Hugging Face within days, and Meta filed DMCA takedowns starting March 6 [T2] DeepLearning.AI, "How Meta's LLaMA NLP Model Leaked", https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/how-metas-llama-nlp-model-leaked/. The leak effectively forced the question Meta would later answer with Llama 2: ship openly, or watch the weights escape anyway.

**Llama 2 (July 2023)** was that answer. Same dense transformer recipe, scaled to 70B, but released under the new Llama 2 Community License — free commercial use for any organisation with under 700M monthly active users, with an explicit prohibition on using the outputs to train other LLMs [T2] Meta, "Llama 2 Community License Agreement", https://ai.meta.com/llama/license/. The Open Source Initiative immediately rejected the framing, arguing that field-of-use restrictions and the 700M MAU clause violate criterion 6 ("no discrimination against persons or groups") and criterion 5 of the Open Source Definition [T2] OSI, "Meta's LLaMa license is not Open Source", https://opensource.org/blog/metas-llama-2-license-is-not-open-source. This is the licensing debate in microcosm: the weights are public, the license is restrictive, and which word you use ("open source", "open weights", "openly available") signals which side you're on.

**Mistral 7B (October 2023)** changed the architectural conversation. Apache 2.0 licensed, it introduced grouped-query attention (GQA) for cheaper inference and sliding-window attention (SWA) — each token attending to W previous-layer tokens, with information propagating k×W tokens after k layers — and outperformed Llama 2 13B across all evaluated benchmarks plus Llama 1 34B on reasoning, math, and code [T1] Jiang et al., "Mistral 7B", arXiv 2310.06825, p. 2, ¶1. Mistral demonstrated that a small Paris startup could ship a model that mattered, under a license that the OSI actually endorses.

**Mixtral 8x7B (January 2024)** was the first sparse-MoE open-weight release to hit the frontier. Architecturally a Mistral 7B decoder where each feed-forward block is replaced by 8 experts with a top-2 router, it has ~45B total parameters but only ~13B active per token, runs a 32k context, and beats Llama 2 70B on most benchmarks at 6× faster inference [T1] Jiang et al., "Mixtral of Experts", arXiv 2401.04088, p. 1, ¶1. Apache 2.0 again. Mixtral popularised MoE outside the closed-model labs and seeded a wave of routed-expert open releases.

**Llama 3 / 3.1 (Meta, July 2024)** went the opposite direction: pure dense transformer, no architectural fireworks, but trained on 15 trillion multilingual tokens at unprecedented scale. The 405B-parameter flagship reached "comparable quality to leading language models such as GPT-4 on a plethora of tasks", with the paper explicitly noting that "performance gains [are] primarily driven by improvements in data quality and diversity as well as increased training scale" [T1] Llama Team, "The Llama 3 Herd of Models", arXiv 2407.21783, p. 2, ¶1. Llama 3.1 405B was the first open-weight model that could plausibly be called frontier.

**Llama 4 (April 2025)** was Meta's first MoE family and first natively multimodal release. Scout (17B active / 16 experts) and Maverick (17B active / 128 experts) were released openly; Behemoth (288B active / ~2T total parameters, 16 experts) was still in training at announcement. All three use early-fusion multimodality for image+text, available via Hugging Face and llama.com [T2] Meta AI, "The Llama 4 herd: The beginning of a new era of natively multimodal AI innovation", https://ai.meta.com/blog/llama-4-multimodal-intelligence/.

## 3. The Chinese surge: Qwen, DeepSeek, and the cost-collapse story

The story stops being a Meta-and-Mistral story in late 2024.

**Qwen2.5 (Alibaba, December 2024)** is the deepest open-weight family by sheer count: pre-trained and instruction-tuned variants at 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B, 14B, 32B, and 72B, scaled from the previous 7T to 18T pre-training tokens, with mixed licensing (most checkpoints Apache 2.0; the 3B and 72B require commercial-arrangement) [T1] Qwen Team, "Qwen2.5 Technical Report", arXiv 2412.15115, p. 2, ¶3. The pattern matters: Alibaba covers every parameter count a developer might want, in a single coherent family, with consistent tooling.

**DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024)** was the cost-collapse moment. A 671B-total/37B-active MoE, with one shared expert and 256 routed experts per layer, top-8 routing, trained on 14.8T tokens. The architectural innovations were dense: Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) compresses the KV cache via low-rank projection; an auxiliary-loss-free load balancing strategy replaces the traditional MoE balance loss; multi-token prediction acts as an auxiliary training objective; and the entire run used FP8 mixed precision — the first validation of FP8 training at this scale [T1] DeepSeek-AI, "DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report", arXiv 2412.19437, p. 1, ¶2. Total cost: 2.788 million H800 GPU-hours, roughly $5.6 million in compute — orders of magnitude below the figures whispered about GPT-4-class training runs [T1] DeepSeek-AI, arXiv 2412.19437, p. 1, ¶3. MIT-licensed.

**DeepSeek-R1 (January 23, 2025)** was the reasoning analogue. Trained with reinforcement learning on top of the V3 base, plus six dense distilled checkpoints based on Qwen and Llama, all under MIT license [T1] DeepSeek-AI, "DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning", arXiv 2501.12948, p. 1, ¶2. The R1 release on Jan 23 triggered the now-famous market reaction: on January 27, 2025, Nvidia fell 18% — roughly $589 billion in market cap evaporated in a single session — on fears that DeepSeek's training-cost numbers undermined the demand thesis for premium GPUs [T3] TechCrunch, "Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang says market got it wrong about DeepSeek's impact", https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/21/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-says-market-got-it-wrong-about-deepseeks-impact/. Marc Andreessen tweeted "DeepSeek R1 is AI's Sputnik moment" [T3] Stanford FSI, "Taking Stock of the DeepSeek Shock", https://cyber.fsi.stanford.edu/publication/taking-stock-deepseek-shock. The shock was not really about R1's quality — it was the realisation that an open-weight, MIT-licensed Chinese model could match o1-class reasoning, and that a "$6M training run" was the new frame in policy debates.

## 4. Small models and data-quality maximalism: Phi and Gemma

While the frontier story is about scale, the parallel story is about how small you can go. Microsoft's **Phi-3-mini (April 2024)** is the canonical example: 3.8B parameters, 3.3T training tokens, 69% MMLU, 8.38 MT-Bench — "rivalling Mixtral 8x7B and GPT-3.5… despite being small enough to be deployed on a phone" [T1] Abdin et al., "Phi-3 Technical Report: A Highly Capable Language Model Locally on Your Phone", arXiv 2404.14219, p. 1, ¶2. The paper is explicit that "the innovation lies entirely in the dataset for training, a scaled-up version of the one used for phi-2, composed of heavily filtered web data and synthetic data" [T1] Abdin et al., arXiv 2404.14219, p. 1, ¶3. Phi-3-small (7B) and phi-3-medium (14B) extended the family to 75% and 78% MMLU respectively. The Phi thesis — data quality, not parameter count — is the small-model wing of the open-weight ecosystem.

Google's **Gemma** family sits between the two. Released February 2024 as the "open" sibling of Gemini and built from "the same research and technology used to create the Gemini models," it covered 2B and 7B sizes [T2] Google Open Source Blog, "Building Open Models Responsibly in the Gemini Era", https://opensource.googleblog.com/2024/02/building-open-models-responsibly-gemini-era.html. Gemma 2 (August 2024) added 9B and 27B variants [T1] Gemma Team, "Gemma 2: Improving Open Language Models at a Practical Size", arXiv 2408.00118, abstract. Gemma 3 (March 2025) followed [T1] Gemma Team, "Gemma 3 Technical Report", arXiv 2503.19786, abstract. Gemma's licensing is the inverse of LLaMA's trajectory: it began under the custom "Gemma Terms of Use" (close to Apache, with field-of-use restrictions) and the line moved toward Apache 2.0 over time as Google responded to ecosystem pressure.

## 5. Hugging Face as infrastructure

None of the above functions without Hugging Face. The Transformers library — Wolf et al., "an open-source library with the goal of opening up advances in NLP to the wider machine learning community… carefully engineered state-of-the art Transformer architectures under a unified API, backed by a curated collection of pretrained models" — was the first piece [T1] Wolf et al., "Transformers: State-of-the-Art Natural Language Processing", arXiv 1910.03771, EMNLP 2020, p. 1, ¶2. It became the lingua franca: load any of these models with `AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(...)` and the architectural specifics — GQA, SWA, MoE routing, MLA — are abstracted away.

The Hub itself is now infrastructure of a kind that didn't previously exist for ML. By late 2024 it hosted over 1.2M models; by 2025 over 2 million, with the first million taking ~1000 days from March 2022 and the second arriving 335 days later [T2] AI World, "Hugging Face's two million models and counting", https://aiworld.eu/story/hugging-faces-two-million-models-and-counting. Adjacent: ~600k datasets, ~150k Spaces (gradio/streamlit demos), ~5M model downloads per day [T3] Worldmetrics, "Hugging Face Statistics: Market Data Report 2026", https://worldmetrics.org/hugging-face-statistics/. A formal study — Castaño et al., "Anatomy of a Machine Learning Ecosystem: 2 Million Models on Hugging Face" — quantifies how concentrated this is in practice: the top 50 entities account for roughly 80% of all downloads [T1] Castaño et al., arXiv 2508.06811, p. 1, ¶2. The Hub is "the GitHub of ML" but with a fatter head and a less-active long tail than the rhetoric suggests.

The four-piece stack — Transformers (modelling), Datasets (loading), Hub (distribution), Spaces (demoing) — is what lets a 7B model from a Paris startup, a 671B MoE from a Hangzhou hedge-fund spinoff, and a 3.8B phone model from Redmond all be loaded with the same five lines of Python.

## 6. Licensing, regulation, and the closed-vs-open debate

Three separate fights run in parallel.

**The licensing fight.** Apache 2.0 (Mistral, Mixtral, Qwen, Gemma 4) is the unambiguously-open end of the spectrum. MIT (DeepSeek-V3, R1) sits next to it. Then come the "community licenses": Llama 2/3/4 are free for almost all commercial use but field-of-use-restricted, MAU-capped, and forbid using outputs to train competing LLMs [T2] Meta, https://ai.meta.com/llama/license/. The OSI's position throughout has been that these are not open source — "Meta's LLaMa license is still not Open Source" — because the OSD requires no field-of-use restrictions and no discrimination against persons or groups [T2] OSI, "Meta's LLaMa license is still not Open Source", https://opensource.org/blog/metas-llama-license-is-still-not-open-source. The community has largely settled on "open weights" as the honest descriptor for this tier.

**The definition fight.** On October 28, 2024, at the All Things Open conference, OSI released Open Source AI Definition v1.0 (OSAID), the result of two years of co-design with Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, Mozilla, the Linux Foundation, and others [T2] OSI press release, "The Open Source Initiative Announces the Release of the Industry's First Open Source AI Definition", https://www.prweb.com/releases/the-open-source-initiative-announces-the-release-of-the-industrys-first-open-source-ai-definition-302288553.html. OSAID requires three things: (1) Data Information sufficient to understand and recreate the training data, (2) complete training and inference Code under OSI-approved licenses, and (3) Parameters under OSI-approved terms [T2] OSI, "The Open Source AI Definition – 1.0", https://opensource.org/ai/open-source-ai-definition. Almost no widely-used model meets it: Llama fails on data, DeepSeek-V3 ships weights but not the training data, Mistral ships weights but not training data, Phi ships weights but not the curation pipeline. OSAID is the explicit rebuke to "open weights = open source" conflation.

**The regulatory fight.** Two regimes set the terms. In the US, Biden's October 2023 Executive Order 14110 directed NTIA to study dual-use foundation models with widely available weights. NTIA's July 2024 report came down on the side of openness: it "recommends the U.S. government actively monitor for potential risks to arise, but refrain from restricting the availability of open model weights for currently available systems" [T2] NTIA, "Dual-Use Foundation Models with Widely Available Model Weights Report", https://www.ntia.gov/programs-and-initiatives/artificial-intelligence/open-model-weights-report.

In the EU, the AI Act creates a tiered regime for General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models, broadly equivalent to "foundation models." The default trigger for GPAI obligations is cumulative training compute of 10²³ FLOPs; the systemic-risk tier triggers at 10²⁵ FLOPs [T2] European Commission, "Guidelines for providers of general-purpose AI models", https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-gpai-providers. Open-source GPAI models are exempt from the technical-documentation, downstream-information, and authorised-representative obligations — but they are not exempt from the requirement to publish a "sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training" or from copyright-policy obligations, and the open-source exemption disappears entirely once a model crosses the 10²⁵ FLOPs systemic-risk threshold [T2] HuggingFace blog (Yacine Jernite), "What Open-Source Developers Need to Know about the EU AI Act's Rules for GPAI Models", https://huggingface.co/blog/yjernite/eu-act-os-guideai. The full GPAI obligations went live August 2, 2025.

The capability-gap question — should we worry about open weights catching up to the frontier? — is empirically the gap is small and tightening. Epoch AI tracks open-weight-vs-closed at a roughly 7-point ECI gap on average, with episodic full closures (DeepSeek-R1 vs o3-mini on MATH Level 5) [T3] Epoch AI, https://epoch.ai/blog/open-models-report. Whether that's a problem (proliferation of dual-use capability) or a feature (decentralisation, reproducibility, on-device privacy) is what the policy debate is actually about.

## 7. What to take away

The open-weights ecosystem in 2026 is a four-part stack: a model lineage (BLOOM → LLaMA → Mistral → Llama 3/4 → Qwen → DeepSeek) with a parallel small-model branch (Phi, Gemma); an infrastructure layer (Hugging Face Transformers + Hub + Datasets + Spaces) that abstracts architectural diversity behind a single API; a licensing spectrum (Apache 2.0 → MIT → community licenses → restricted weights) that the OSI's OSAID has formalised; and a bifurcating regulatory regime (NTIA monitoring, EU AI Act tiered exemptions). The capability gap to closed models exists but is months, not years, and Chinese labs collapsed both the capability and the cost gap in a single quarter. The interesting question for the next round is not "will open weights catch up?" but "what happens when they do?"
